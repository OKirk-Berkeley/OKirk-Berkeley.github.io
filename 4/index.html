In this project I stitch photographs into mosaics by recovering projective transforms (homographies) between views, warping images into a shared coordinate frame, and blending the overlap. Part A is fully manual: I click correspondences myself. Part B will automate that correspondence step.

A.1 Shoot the Pictures

I captured overlapping photos with a roughly fixed center of projection, rotating the camera between shots so the motion is approximately projective. I aimed for substantial overlap (around half the field of view) and took each pair in quick succession so lighting and scene content stayed stable. Below are the image sets used for mosaicing.

A.2 Recover Homographies

To find our homography matrix, we are searching for some $3 \times 3$ matrix $H$ where every input point maps to its corresponding output point:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} w x' \\ w y' \\ w \end{bmatrix} = H \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} $$

If $H$ looks like

$$ H = \begin{bmatrix} a & b & c \\ d & e & f \\ g & h & 1 \end{bmatrix} $$

we have the system of equations

$$ \begin{aligned} w x' &= a x + b y + c \\ w y' &= d x + e y + f \\ w &= g x + h y + 1 \end{aligned} $$

or, equivalently,

$$ \begin{aligned} x' &= \frac{a x + b y + c}{g x + h y + 1} \\[0.5em] y' &= \frac{d x + e y + f}{g x + h y + 1} \end{aligned} $$

With some arithmetic, we can rewrite this as

$$ \begin{aligned} x' &= a x + b y + c - g x x' - h y x' \\ y' &= d x + e y + f - g x y' - h y y' \end{aligned} $$

For each $x$-coordinate correspondence, we can then solve

$$ \begin{bmatrix} x & y & 1 & 0 & 0 & 0 & -x x' & -y x' \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} a \\ b \\ c \\ d \\ e \\ f \\ g \\ h \end{bmatrix} = x' $$

and for each $y$-coordinate correspondence

$$ \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 & x & y & 1 & -x y' & -y y' \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} a \\ b \\ c \\ d \\ e \\ f \\ g \\ h \end{bmatrix} = y' $$

For multiple points we can then solve the matrix equation

$$ \begin{bmatrix} x_1 & y_1 & 1 & 0 & 0 & 0 & -x_1 x'_1 & -y_1 x'_1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & x_1 & y_1 & 1 & -x_1 y'_1 & -y_1 y'_1 \\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots \\ x_n & y_n & 1 & 0 & 0 & 0 & -x_n x'_n & -y_n x'_n \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & x_n & y_n & 1 & -x_n y'_n & -y_n y'_n \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} a \\ b \\ c \\ d \\ e \\ f \\ g \\ h \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} x'_1 \\ y'_1 \\ \vdots \\ x'_n \\ y'_n \end{bmatrix} $$

A.3 Warp the Images

Warping uses inverse mapping: for every destination pixel, apply H−1 to find the corresponding source location, then interpolate. This avoids holes that forward-warping leaves. I estimate the output canvas by transforming the four source corners through H and taking their axis-aligned bounding box. I implemented two interpolators from scratch — nearest neighbor (fast, blockier edges) and bilinear (smoother, preferred for mosaics). Before mosaicing, I tested the pipeline by rectifying known rectangles: click the four corners of a planar object, then map them to a rectangle. Results for both interpolators are shown below.

Nearest neighbor is cheaper but introduces jagged artifacts under strong warps. Bilinear averages the four surrounding source pixels, which softens those edges and generally looks better for both rectification and mosaicing, at a modest extra cost.

A.4 Blend Images into a Mosaic

To build a mosaic I leave one image as the reference (identity homography) and warp the others into that frame. All images are warped onto a shared canvas sized to the union of their projected bounding boxes. Instead of overwriting, I blend with distance-to-edge feathering: each source pixel gets a weight equal to its distance to the nearest image border (normalized to [0, 1]). Warped weights travel with the image, and the final pixel is a weighted average of all contributions.
