In this project I stitch photographs into mosaics by recovering projective transforms (homographies) between views, warping images into a shared coordinate frame, and blending the overlap. Part A is fully manual: I pick correspondences myself. Part B will automate that correspondence step.

A.1 Shoot the Pictures

I captured overlapping photos with a (roughly) fixed center of projection, rotating the camera between shots so the motion is projective. I aimed for substantial overlap (around half the field of view) and took each pair in quick succession so lighting and scene content stayed stable. Below are the image sets used for mosaicing.

A.2 Recover Homographies

To find our homography matrix, we are searching for some $3 \times 3$ matrix $H$ where every input point maps to its corresponding output point:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} w x' \\ w y' \\ w \end{bmatrix} = H \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} $$

If $H$ looks like

$$ H = \begin{bmatrix} a & b & c \\ d & e & f \\ g & h & 1 \end{bmatrix} $$

we have the system of equations

$$ \begin{aligned} w x' &= a x + b y + c \\ w y' &= d x + e y + f \\ w &= g x + h y + 1 \end{aligned} $$

or, equivalently,

$$ \begin{aligned} x' &= \frac{a x + b y + c}{g x + h y + 1} \\[0.5em] y' &= \frac{d x + e y + f}{g x + h y + 1} \end{aligned} $$

With some arithmetic, we can rewrite this as

$$ \begin{aligned} x' &= a x + b y + c - g x x' - h y x' \\ y' &= d x + e y + f - g x y' - h y y' \end{aligned} $$

For each $x$-coordinate correspondence, we can then solve

$$ \begin{bmatrix} x & y & 1 & 0 & 0 & 0 & -x x' & -y x' \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} a \\ b \\ c \\ d \\ e \\ f \\ g \\ h \end{bmatrix} = x' $$

and for each $y$-coordinate correspondence

$$ \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 & x & y & 1 & -x y' & -y y' \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} a \\ b \\ c \\ d \\ e \\ f \\ g \\ h \end{bmatrix} = y' $$

For multiple points we can then solve the matrix equation

$$ \begin{bmatrix} x_1 & y_1 & 1 & 0 & 0 & 0 & -x_1 x'_1 & -y_1 x'_1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & x_1 & y_1 & 1 & -x_1 y'_1 & -y_1 y'_1 \\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots \\ x_n & y_n & 1 & 0 & 0 & 0 & -x_n x'_n & -y_n x'_n \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & x_n & y_n & 1 & -x_n y'_n & -y_n y'_n \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} a \\ b \\ c \\ d \\ e \\ f \\ g \\ h \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} x'_1 \\ y'_1 \\ \vdots \\ x'_n \\ y'_n \end{bmatrix} $$

Let's look at an example on some of the images that are linked via projective transform. For these two images, we can take the following points that appear in both images to define a correspondence. Note that since there are 8 corresponding points, the above strategy will produce 16 equations in order to solve for only 8 unknowns. As a result, we use the least squares solution, which produces a more stable result than exactly calculating a homography on only 4 correspondences (8 equations).

A.3 Warp the Images

To warp an image using H, we multiply every pixel by H to find its corresponding pixel in the output. These output destinations sometimes do not fall cleanly onto new pixel locations. For example, some input pixels may map between two pixels, or two inputs may map to the same output destination. That can leave holes in the output where no input pixel was mapped. To counter this, we use inverse mapping. This means that for every destination pixel, we apply H−1 to find the corresponding source location, then interpolate. We can estimate the size and shape of the output canvas by first forward mapping the four source corners through H, then inverse mapping every pixel within these output corners.

When output pixels don't map cleanly to input pixels, interpolation is needed. For this project, I implemented two interpolation techniques. The first is nearest-neighbor interpolation: when an output pixel maps between multiple input pixels, it receives the value of the closest neighbor. Bilinear interpolation, on the other hand, takes a weighted average of the surrounding input pixels, weighted by how close the mapped location is to each.

Here, I use inverse mapping with both nearest-neighbor and bilinear interpolation to rectify images. Image rectification uses homographies to transform input images onto a given plane. To show how powerful this is, I've rectified images with known square or rectangular objects onto a plane where the camera looks at them orthogonally. To do this, I computed H using the four corners of the rectangular object (selected by hand) as input points (x and y) and four hard-coded rectangular points as the output points (x' and y'). The first two images have been cropped to just show the rectified portion while the last is shown in its entirety to show how this process distorts the rest of the image.

Nearest neighbor is cheaper but introduces jagged artifacts under strong warps. The doubles of certain tiles in the first image are an example of that. Bilinear averages the four surrounding source pixels, which softens those edges and generally looks better for both rectification and mosaicing, at an extra cost. Since the only extra cost to bilinear comes in the interpolation phase and does not affect the more expensive actual computing of H, this is generally a modest extra cost.

A.4 Blend Images into a Mosaic

To build a mosaic I leave one image as the reference and warp the other to fit it. All images are warped onto a shared canvas sized to the union of their projected bounding boxes. To reduce edge artifacts like abrupt cutoffs or ghosting at the point where images meet, I give each pixel in each image a weight. The weight is 1 at the center of each image and linearly decreases to zero by the edges. The weights of pixels in warped images come from the weights of their corresponding input pixels. In the final mosaic, each pixel is a weighted average of the corresponding pixels from the two images (one of which is already warped to fit the other).

But wait, this image doesn't look as clean. In the area where the two images overlap, there is clear ghosting. It is especially clear with the bushes in the back, which appear to have doubles. To fix this, we may need to use a more sophisticated image blending technique between the two images. We can revisit Laplacian Blending, our old friend from Project 2! This is done by splitting each image into high-pass and low-pass versions and combining them differently. For the high-pass images, I use a hard border: each pixel comes from whichever input has the higher weight. For the low-pass images, I still use a weighted average of the feather weights, but those weights are blurred with the same filter used on the images. Adding the high-pass and low-pass blends gives the final mosaic.

Part B: Feature Matching for Autostitching

Part B replaces the hand-picked correspondences from Part A with an automatic pipeline. This pipeline attempts to find corresponding corners in both images by first using Harris corner detection to select all corners in each image. It then uses Adaptive Non-Maximal Suppression to reduce the number of corners in each image to a spatially distributed set that responded strongest to the Harris corner detection function. Features are extracted from each point, and we attempt to match points across the images by finding correspondences between their features. Finally, a homography is computed from these matching points and the images are warped as in Part A. Each of the steps is described in more detail below.

B.1 Harris Corner Detection

Harris corner detection finds the points in an image where the absolute value of the derivative of the image is high in many directions. This algorithm capitalizes on the fact that while edges have a strong derivative response perpendicular to the edge, corners must have that strong response perpendicular to the direction of each edge making up that corner. The Harris corner detection algorithm uses this to assign a 'corner strength' to each pixel. Below are all the corners this algorithm identified in my two input images.

All Harris corners

Why are there so many?

These examples are computed by finding pixels that respond the strongest to the Harris function within a 1-pixel radius. This allows computed corners to be right next to each other, which is OK because the next step, Adaptive Non-Maximal Supression (ANMS), will reduce the number of corners we have. If we were not using ANMS, we could ensure corners were more spread out by selecting pixels that have the strongest Harris response within a 10-pixel radius. This is what that set of corners would look like:

We want to reduce the number of corners to a manageable number, keeping the pixels with the strongest corner response without letting them all cluster around the densest part of the image. To do this, we order our corners by the distance from each corner to the nearest point that is significantly stronger than it. By selecting the first n points from this list, we keep both the strongest points and points that are far from each other, creating a nice spatial distribution over our image. B.2 Feature Descriptor Extraction

We can then create a feature for each point that can be compared to the features of points in the other image. If two features match, the points likely correspond to the same object. To construct a feature, we sample an 8x8 swath from the 40x40 pixel area surrounding the point. We then bias and gain normalize the captured 8x8 patch. This captures information from the whole 40x40 pixel region while downsampling to only 8x8 pixels in order to reduce feature size.

B.3 Feature Matching

Each feature in one image is then compared to every feature in the other using L2 distance. Not every point will have a perfect correspondence in the other image's set of points, so we must select which features are considered matching. Since we expect a feature to be substantially closer to its true match than any other feature in the other image, we can decide whether a feature's match is valid based on how much smaller the distance to its best match in the other image is than the distance to its second-best match. B.4 RANSAC for Robust Homography

Finally, we're ready to compute the homography. Since our remaining points may still have faulty correspondences, we use Random Sample Consensus (RANSAC). First, we randomly select 4 correspondences and compute a homography using them. We then determine how many other correspondences are consistent with this homography; these are called inliers. After doing this many times, we select the largest set of inliers over our runs. We compute our final homography by performing least squares regression over all these inliers, as described in Part A.
