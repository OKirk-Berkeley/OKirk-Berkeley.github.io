Project 1

Single Scale

First Attempt: NCC over the Whole Image.

Images in this collection are digital scans of black-and-white exposures taken on glass plates using red, blue, and green filters. To align the different color channels of each image, I matched the red and green channels to the blue channel by exhaustively searching over displacements ranging from −15 to +15 pixels in both directions. To determine how well each displacement fit, I used Normalized Cross-Correlation (NCC). To do this, I treated each image as a 1D vector, subtracted its mean, and normalized it. I used the dot product of these two vectors to produce each image's final NCC score. This score represents the similarity between the two vectors, with the highest score representing the displacement that made the images the most similar when compared on a pixel-by-pixel basis. In my first attempt, I calculated the NCC over the entire image, including the misaligned color borders. These borders had a disproportionate influence on the calculated alignment, resulting in the misaligned images shown here.

To fix this, I cropped the outermost 10% from each side of an image before calculating the NCC. The reason NCC works well for aligning these images is that we expect the different color channels of each image to be bright or dark in the same places, on aggregate. Because we subtract the mean of each vector from each of its elements, NCC is less sensitive to differences in the average brightness between color channels. The vectors are also normalized so that differences in the overall intensity scale do not disproportionately affect the calculation. Cropping the outermost pixels allows the similarity in pixel intensity to be measured over the region where corresponding pixels are more likely to represent the same part of the scene, without the misaligned borders affecting the final displacement.

Image Pyramid

For the larger .tif images, two problems presented themselves. The first was that the displacements needed to align each color channel were sometimes in the hundreds of pixels. The second was that, because the images were so much larger, all the calculations took much longer. These two factors combined made the exhaustive search over a small pixel range used earlier completely untenable.

To solve this, I used image pyramids. The basic idea is to shrink the images to much smaller versions and quickly calculate an estimate of the displacement needed to align the full-sized images by scaling up the displacement needed to align the smaller images. I then progressively refine that estimate by making the images progressively larger and exhaustively searching a very small area surrounding the estimated displacement. At each step, the estimate is refined.

In more detail, I scaled the images down by halving both their width and height (being careful to use anti-aliasing) until their minimum dimension was less than or equal to 200 pixels. At this scale, I would exhaustively search a 15-pixel radius. This may not sound like much, but a 1-pixel shift at this scale is equal to a 2n-pixel shift in the original image, where n is the number of times the image was downsized. Since smaller images aren't downsized as much, they end up searching a much smaller pixel displacement region than their larger counterparts, which is useful because smaller images require smaller displacements to be properly aligned. In practice, the JPEG files were only reduced once, so they ended up searching the equivalent of a 30-pixel radius of possible displacements at the smallest scale. The TIFF files were shrunk 5 times, so they ended up searching the equivalent of a 480-pixel radius of possible displacements at the smallest scale. After calculating these displacement estimates, the images were resized to one size larger, and the estimates were refined by searching a 2-pixel radius region centered at the previous displacement estimate. The highest-scoring displacement was then used as the estimate around which the next 2-pixel search was centered at the next size up. This process was repeated until the final displacement was calculated at the full image size.

My Images

The Emir Problem

This algorithm was unable to successfully align the color channels for this photograph. Why? The algorithm is based on the assumption that the different color channels will all have similar values at the same point when they are properly aligned. An NCC score is, at its core, simply a measure of how similar two images are. Because the Emir's robe appears so differently in each color channel, the proper alignment actually doesn't involve the different color channels having the most similar brightness values at every pixel. Since my algorithm assumes the correct alignment will be the one that produces the strongest pixel-by-pixel correlation between the images, this is enough to cause my algorithm to fail. NCC protects against different color channels having different average brightness values by adjusting each image's mean brightness to 0 before comparing two images, but it can't protect against images with the same brightness values being bright in different places.
