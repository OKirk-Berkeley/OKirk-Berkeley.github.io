Single Scale

First Attempt: NCC over the Whole Image.

Images in this collection are digital scans of black-and-white exposures taken on glass plates taken using red, blue, and green filters. To align the different colorways of the image, I matched the red and green colorways to the blue by exauhstively searching a 15 by 15 pixel displacement. To determine how well each displacement fit, I used Normalized Cross-Correlation (NCC). To do this, I treated each image as a 1D vector, and subtracted its mean and normalized it. I then compared the dot products of the two vectors. In my first attempt, I took the NCC over the whole image, including the misaligned color boarders. These boarders overcontributed to the final calculated alignment, resulting in these misaligned images.

To fix this, I cropped the outermost 10% of each side of an image before calculating the NCC. The reason NCC works so well to align the images is that we expect the different colorways of each image to be bright or dark in the same places (on aggregate). Because we subtract the mean of each vector from each of the elements, even if one colorway is much brighter than another on average, their dot products will be close under NCC. The vectors are also normalized so that high-value pixels don't dominate the calculation. Cropping the outermost pixels allows the similarity in pixel intensity to be measured over the part of the image we care about without the outliers produced by the out of synch borders of the different colorways affecting the final displacement.

Image Pyramid

For the larger .tif images, two problems presented themseleves. The first was that the displacements needed to align each color were sometimes in the hundreds. The second was that because the images were so much longer, all the calculations took much longer. These two facts combined made the exahustive search over a small pixel range used earlier completley untennable.

To solve this, I used image pyramids. The basic idea is to shrink the images to much smaller versions and quickly calculate an estimate of the displacement needed to align the full-sized images by scaling up the displacement needed to align the smaller images. Then, to progressivley refine that estimate by making the images progressivley larger and exauhstively searching a very small area surrounding the estimated displacement. At each step, the estimate is refined.

In more detail, I scaled the images down by halving both their width and height (being careful to use anti-aliasing) until their minimum dimmension was less than or equal to 200 pixels. At this scale, I would exauhstively search a 15x15 pixel radius. This may not sound like much, but a 1 pixel shift at this scale is equal to a 2n pixel shift in the original image (where n is the number of times the image was downsized). Since smaller images aren't downsized as much, they end up searching a much smaller pixel displacement region than their larger counterparts, which is perfect, as smaller images require smaller displacements to be properly aligned. In practice, the JPEG files were only reduced once, so ended up searching the equivilent of a 30x30 pixel displacement at the smallest scale. The TIFF files were shrunk 5 times, so ended up searching the equivilent of a 480x480 pixel dispalcement at the smallest scale. After calculating these displacement estimates, the images were resized to one size larger and the estimates were refined by searching a 2x2 pixel region centered at the previous dispalcement estimate. The highest scoring displacement was then used as the estimate the next 2x2 search was centered around at the next size up. This process repeated until the final displacement was calculated at the full image size.

The Emir Problem

This algorithm was unable to successfully align the color chanels for this photograph. Why? The algorithm is based on the assumption that the different color chanels will all have similar values at the same point when they are properly aligned. An NCC score is, at its core, simply a measure of how similar two images are. Because the Emir's robe appears so differently in each color chanel, the proper alignment actually doesn't involve the different color chanels having the most similar brightness value at every pixel. Since my algorithm assumes the correct alignment will be the one that matches the pixel-by-pixel brightness values of each images the best, this is enough to cause my algorithm to fail. NCC protects against different color chanels having different average brightness values by adjusting each image's average brightness to 0 before comparing two images, but it can't protect against images with the same brightness value being bright in different places.
